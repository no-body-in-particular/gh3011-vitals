# Saturation on this sensor: not possible, and why

**SpO2 has been removed from this software.** The sensor cannot measure it in this configuration,
the vendor's own algorithm cannot either, and the vendor shipped the feature disabled. What follows
is the evidence, because the same ideas will otherwise be tried again.

## The final test

A finger meter against our ratio, watch strapped firmly - firm contact occludes venous pulsation and
is the standard fix for exactly this failure mode:

    pass  ac1  ac2  ac2/ac1    R
      2    79  197   2.49    0.403
      5    73  195   2.67    0.378
      7    86  225   2.62    0.383
      9   114  235   2.06    0.485
     12    70  193   2.76    0.366

    meter    99 -> 87, twelve points
    R        0.366 to 0.485, sd 0.035, no correspondence with the hold

Twelve points of desaturation required R to move from 0.43 to about 0.82. It stayed inside a band of
0.119 and wandered within it independently of the wearer's breathing.

Every prerequisite was met, and each had been missing from an earlier attempt:

* a genuine wavelength pair - `ac2/ac1` of 2.1 to 2.8, where the shipped configuration gives 1.0
* the vendor's own scale - our R of 0.43 against their 0.496 for the 98% a meter agrees with
* firm contact, which removed the bimodality that spoiled the previous run
* a deep dip, twelve points, the largest of five attempts
* a still wearer throughout, `gssd` in single figures

## Why it cannot work here

**One photodiode.** The datasheet gives three LED drivers and a single PD, and the library confirms
it: two logic channels, permuted onto three physical LEDs by `emLedLogicChannelMap`. Commercial
wrist oximetry uses several photodiodes at different spacings, because separating arterial from
venous pulsation and correcting optical path length needs spatial diversity. With one detector there
are two numbers and more than two unknowns.

**The vendor could not do it either.** Their daemon, with their curve, their coefficients and their
configuration, was run through a seven point desaturation: 189 of 196 reports read exactly 98, and
their own confidence figure never flagged it. And `RUN_MODE_SPO2_DET` is behind a flag that is zero
on this watch, with the sensor HAL only ever sending commands 4, 5 and 6 - so nothing on this
product has ever asked for a saturation. That reads as a decision rather than an oversight.

**What the ratio is actually measuring.** It is stable, repeatable to 0.004 at rest, on the right
scale, and inert. That is the signature of a venous-and-arterial mix: venous blood is desaturated by
definition, and a ratio set by the mixture sits wherever the mixture puts it regardless of arterial
saturation. Firm contact reduced the contact-driven bimodality without making the ratio respond,
which is consistent with the arterial component simply being too small a part of what is measured.

## The one thing not closed

Every tracking run tested one formulation: peak-to-baseline, `(AC1/DC1)/(AC2/DC2)`. Log-ratio,
per-beat medians, peak-to-trough and a measured dark level are all different enough to be worth
trying, and testing them needs raw samples spanning a desaturation.

Three attempts at that capture failed, and the reasons are worth recording because they are all
fixable:

**The pairing does not survive a long run.** `0x0136` is a one-shot written before sampling, the
current loop writes zero over it on every adjustment, and across 110 and 150 seconds the pairing is
eventually lost - both long captures ended with `ac2` below `ac1`, where 26-second passes hold it at
2.2 times. Raw capture through a hold therefore needs several short passes.

**And `RAWDUMP` cannot be de-interleaved offline.** Eight short passes were captured successfully,
with the pairing held throughout, and the analysis still could not reproduce `ppgd`'s own ratio from
its own samples: correlations of +0.27, -0.04, -0.22 and -0.19 for the four formulations, against
absolute values of 0.83 to 1.6 where `ppgd` reported 0.40 to 0.51.

The reason is the dump format. `RAWDUMP` writes a flat stream of three-byte samples with no burst
boundaries, while `ppgd` pairs bytes within each burst - `k += 6` from the burst start. Bursts are
`lvl * 3` bytes and `lvl` varies, so a burst with an odd sample count flips the channel phase and an
even/odd split scrambles the two channels from that point on. `ppgd` is immune; anything reading the
dump is not.

This also means the earlier offline analyses in this file that split `RAWDUMP` by even and odd index
- the period checks, the Goertzel amplitude comparisons - were relying on the phase happening to
hold. The period-2 result was strong enough to survive it; the ratio numbers were not.

**They have now been tested, and none of them tracks either.**

`RAWDUMP` was changed to write paired frames - `c1 c2` a line, with a burst marker - so the file no
longer has to be de-interleaved. `tools/gh3011/ratio-lab.py` reads that and reproduces `ppgd`'s own
number before offering an opinion, which took four attempts and is the only reason to believe the
rest of what it prints:

    a single Goertzel over the whole pass    R 1.05     ppgd said 0.522
    the same, per beat                       R 1.10
    band_amp as ppgd computes it             R 0.404    ppgd said 0.409

The difference is that `band_amp` averages six-second windows at 50% overlap. A wearer's rate moves
with their breathing, so a fine frequency bin over a long window misses most of the pulse: a one bpm
shift moved the measured amplitude by a factor of two. Coarse bins, averaged, do not.

With that validated, eight short passes were captured through a hold that took the meter from 99 to
91 - eight points, or 0.262 of ratio on the vendor's scale:

    formulation              min     max   range
    band_amp (reference)   0.218   0.451   0.233
    second harmonic        0.170   0.234   0.065
    peak-to-trough         1.286   1.377   0.091
    log-ratio              1.246   1.345   0.099
    per-beat median        1.242   1.427   0.185
    raw DC (no pedestal)   0.214   0.442   0.228

None of them peaks during the hold. The three time-domain forms - peak-to-trough, log-ratio and the
per-beat median - do not depend on the frequency estimate at all, and they move by about a third of
what is required, in no particular relation to the wearer's breathing. `band_amp` and the raw-DC
variant move further but their maximum falls at pass 3, before the hold.

The raw captures are kept under `docs/data/hold-raw/` so this does not have to be asked of a wearer
again. Anything else anyone wants to try can be tried against them.

# The GH3011, as this code drives it

A Goodix GH3011 optical sensor: a green LED and a red/infrared pair, two photodiode channels, and
a FIFO. It sits on i2c bus 2 at address 0x14, reached through `/dev/gh_tools` rather than an i2c
device node, because the kernel driver owns the bus and offers a passthrough.

This describes what the registers do and how the three things this code needs - a start, a
measurement, and a wear check - are performed. It is written from captures of the vendor daemon on
the wire and from measurements taken against a cuff and a hand count.

## Reaching the part

    /dev/gh_tools

    ioctl  0xc0084704   i2c passthrough, an i2c_rdwr_ioctl_data of struct msg
    ioctl  0x40044702   power, 1 or 0
    ioctl  0x40044707   interrupt enable, 1 or 0
    ioctl  0x00004701   wait for an interrupt, blocking
    ioctl  0x40044706   driver init
    ioctl  0x40184709   publish a result to the framework's sensor

A register write is a four-byte message: two bytes of big-endian address, two of value. A read is a
two-message transfer, the address written and then two bytes read back. Some writes are three bytes
- a command, described below.

Two cautions, both learned expensively:

**Read the chip id alongside anything that matters.** `0x0028` reads `0x0031`. When it does not, the
passthrough is returning the previous read's value rather than register contents, and everything
read in that window is fiction rather than data.

**Time the interrupt wait before trusting it.** `0x00004701` returns success without an interrupt
when the part is not configured - in about a second out of a twenty second window, and identically
with the interrupt disabled. A wait that returns early did not wait.

## Commands

Written as `dd dd <cmd>`, three bytes, to no register:

    c0   halt
    c1   start
    c3   arm a read of a running part
    c4   stop

A running part returns zero for every register in the space until `c3` is issued.

## Registers

### Timing

| register | meaning |
|---|---|
| `0x0016` | sample rate divisor: rate = 32768 / value. `051e` is 25 Hz, `0147` is 100 Hz |
| `0x0002` | written `fe30` before an auto-detect start; the part alters what is written |

Green runs at 25 Hz and red/infrared at 100. A heart rate needs no more than 25 - 200 bpm is
3.3 Hz - but the systolic upstroke behind a blood pressure is four samples at that rate, so only
the pressure asks for 100.

### Light

| register | meaning |
|---|---|
| `0x0080` | LED drive, per slot |
| `0x0082` | LED drive, per slot |
| `0x0084` | integration and drive selection |
| `0x0118` | gain. `2828` for a measurement, `1f69` in the auto-detect table |
| `0x011a` | gain, second channel |
| `0x0136` | slot LED current |
| `0x0186` | slot enable |

### Slots and sampling

| register | meaning |
|---|---|
| `0x0100`-`0x0116` | per-slot ADC windows, six pairs |
| `0x011c`-`0x0120` | per-slot thresholds |
| `0x0126`, `0x0128` | slot configuration |
| `0x012a`, `0x012c` | slot timing |
| `0x0130`-`0x0134` | per-slot LED selection |
| `0x0180`, `0x0182` | analogue front end |
| `0x018a`, `0x018c`, `0x0194` | trims, read then written back modified |

### Data and status

| register | meaning |
|---|---|
| `0x004a` | FIFO level, counted in three-byte samples |
| `0xaaaa` | the FIFO, three bytes big-endian per sample |
| `0x0008` | interrupt status. Bit 0 new data, bit 4 wear event, `0x20` chip reset |
| `0x0028` | chip id, `0x0031` |
| `0x0022` | bit 0 is set during init |
| `0x0020` | cleared during init |
| `0x0064`, `0x006a`, `0x006c` | an OTP window: address into `0x0064`, pulse `0x006a` with 1 then 0, read the word from `0x006c` |

`0x004a` counts samples and not frames. Two channels are interleaved, so a frame is two samples and
an odd level is half a frame.

### 0x0136, the LED drive

Two fields in one register: the **low byte drives channel 1 and the high byte channel 2**. Measured
with the gain frozen, as the level above the 3145728 pedestal:

| low byte | ch1 level | high byte | ch2 level |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0x08 | 64,867 | 0x00 | 64,850 |
| 0x10 | 195,933 | 0x0d | 326,895 |
| 0x20 | 326,974 | 0x1a | 588,781 |
| 0x30 | 457,974 | 0x7f | 1,899,082 |
| 0x40 | 588,997 | | |
| 0x60 | 862,458 | | |

So `level ~= 65536 + (byte / 0x10) * 131072`, linear and independent per channel - `0d20` is the
balanced pair the measurement uses, and `0d60` gives channel 1 about 2.6 times that. The steps are
131072 exactly, which is 0x20000.

It was found by sweeping with FREEZEGAIN and reading the level rather than the raw DC. Without
freezing the gain nothing appears to happen at all: the search compensates for the extra light by
backing the gain off, and the DC lands within half a percent of where it started whatever the LEDs
are told to do. That is why an earlier sweep concluded the register did nothing.

#### The amplitude itself is what moves

Everything above chases a setting, and the setting is not the problem. The dedicated ratio pass,
run four times through the daemon on a still wrist over about ten minutes:

    ac1 51.0   ac2 227.2   R 0.429
    ac1 11.2   ac2  17.2   R 1.124   weak=1
    ac1 11.9   ac2  17.6   R 1.039   weak=1

The pulse amplitude fell by a factor of five on one channel and thirteen on the other, on the same
path, the same wrist and the same registers, minutes apart. R follows it from 0.43 to 1.12 - across
the whole physiological range and out the other side.

That is the reason every promising setting in this file stopped reproducing, including the ones
recorded here as corrections to earlier ones. It is not the gain search, not the LED drive, not the
daemon's path versus a direct run: those all change R, but none of them changes it as much as
simply measuring again a few minutes later does.

A calibration needs the instrument to give the same answer twice. Until the amplitude is stable -
or until enough measurements are averaged that its variation is beaten down, which the drift over
hours may make impossible - there is nothing to calibrate, and a fingertip meter does not help.

#### What raising it does, and what it does not

It works, in the sense that the amplitudes rise and the ratio stops being impossible. With the gain
search left alone and the wrist still:

| 0x0136 | ac1 | ac2 | R |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0d20 (as shipped) | 4 | 9 | 0.454 |
| 0d40 | 8 | 26 | 0.343 |
| 0d60 | 10 | 35 | 0.294 |
| 1f60 | 64 | 72 | 0.917 |

That table is wrong, and an interleaved A/B says so. Run alternately against the shipped setting on
a still wrist, four of each:

    0d20   ac1=16 ac2=37 R 0.481      ac1=31 ac2=72 R 0.466
    1f60   ac1=0  ac2=25 R 0.001      ac1=1 ac2=153 R 0.001

**Raising the drive collapses the red channel**, and R with it. The 64/72 run above was a lucky
sample, and reading a direction out of a single measurement is the mistake this file already
records three times. The balance it appeared to achieve is not the goal either - the note further
down measured that directly: balanced channels gave R 1.855 where an asymmetric pair gave 0.736,
because a ratio between two channels reading the same thing is not a ratio.

What the shipped setting does on a still wrist, over nine runs: R of 0.299, 0.415, 0.466 and 0.481,
with five of the nine failing to find a rate at all. A spread of 0.18 against the 0.2 a six point
desaturation would move it, and a failure rate over half. Better than the 0.35 measured earlier in
the day with the daemon competing for the sensor, and still not enough to calibrate against.

Worth noting that the same wrist read 1.4 to 1.8 through the daemon's two-pass path that morning
and 0.3 to 0.5 through direct runs that afternoon. Whatever accounts for that is worth knowing
before any of these numbers are trusted as a baseline.

The variance most likely comes from the gain search landing somewhere different each run, since the
light is now fixed and the amplitudes are not. Freezing it is the obvious answer and does not work:
FREEZEGAIN with 1f60 puts the levels at 851,022 and 1,899,183 - plenty of light - and reports
amplitudes of 1 and 1. It does the same at the default drive. Whatever the gain search does that
matters, holding the register still does not reproduce it.

So: more light is available, it moves R into the range physiology allows, and it is not yet stable
enough to calibrate against. Finding why a frozen gain has no pulse is the next thing worth doing,
because that is the control that would make any of this repeatable.

### Wear detection

| register | meaning |
|---|---|
| `0x00c0` | bit 0 enables the detector, bit 8 is the answer: clear means on a wrist |
| `0x00c2`, `0x00c6` | per-channel maximum, `ffff` |
| `0x00c4`, `0x00c8` | per-channel threshold. `034b` for the rate mode, `0528` for saturation |
| `0x00ca`, `0x00cc` | detector timing |
| `0x00ce`, `0x00d4` | detector threshold. `0251` for the rate mode, `042e` for saturation |
| `0x00d8`, `0x00dc` | detector configuration |
| `0x00d0`, `0x00d6`, `0x00da`, `0x00de` | zero |

The vendor's configuration table gives the enable's address as `0x10c0`. That is `0x00c0` with a
flag in the top nibble which this passthrough does not decode; written there it does nothing and
reads back zero. Write `0x00c0`.

## Starting the part

The same shape for a measurement and for a wear check, and neither works without all of it.

1. Power on, then halt.
2. Read the id, the rate and the status.
3. Write `0x0182` and `0x0180`, then read three OTP words through the `0x0064` window - `0x20`,
   `0x22` and `0x24`. Nothing visible is done with them; they are trims the part applies itself.
4. Read and write back `0x0194`, `0x018a`, and write `0x018c`.
5. Clear `0x0020`, and set bit 0 of `0x0022` by read-modify-write.
6. Stop.
7. Halt, and write the configuration table for the mode.
8. **Write six of those registers again with different values.** The table is most of the
   configuration and not all of it, and a part configured only from the table never produces an
   interrupt. For the auto-detect mode: `0x0084` = `0020`, `0x0118` = `2828`, `0x0136` = `0d20`,
   `0x0080` = `0205`, `0x0082` = `00c2`, `0x0186` = `0001`.
9. Write `0x00c0` = `0001` and `0x0002` = `fe30`.
10. Start.

`adtwear.c` performs exactly this; `seq.h` holds the equivalent for a measurement, generated from a
capture rather than transcribed.

## Reading a measurement

Poll `0x004a` for the FIFO level and read `0xaaaa`. Do not enable the interrupt for this: the
kernel driver services the part and drains the FIFO before userspace can, so an enabled interrupt
costs the data it was meant to announce.

Each sample is a 24-bit count with a large pedestal. The pedestal is not a constant - it is
`floor(dc / 0x100000) * 0x100000` for that channel, and treating it as a fixed number was wrong by
a factor that varied with the gain.

## Reading a wear event

The opposite: the wear answer only exists as an interrupt.

Arm the detector as above, then block on the wait ioctl. When it returns, halt and read `0x00c0`.
Bit 8 clear means on a wrist. Do not require bit 4 of `0x0008` first - the kernel's own handler
reads and clears the status before userspace gets there, so testing it discards good answers.

A window that closes with no interrupt means the watch is not being worn. Measured on this part:
the event arrives in about a second on a wrist including a still one, and not at all off it, five
runs each way with no overlap. Handling the watch off the wrist does fire it, which is correct -
skin on the sensor is skin on the sensor.

## The vendor gets 98% from this hardware

Their daemon was put back in the init slot and left to run. Over 65 measurements on a wrist whose
fingertip meter read 98 to 99, it reported:

    98%  x49        heart rate 52-58
    97%  x12        (ours reads 47-54 on the same wrist - the pulse is not the problem)
    95%  x4

So the sensor, the LEDs and this wrist can produce a saturation worth publishing. Nothing about the
instability we get is forced by the hardware, and the algorithm is not the difference either - it is
110 - 25R for them exactly as it is for us.

The difference is the configuration. Read off the running chip with `dumpregs` while their daemon
was measuring, against what our own start sequence leaves:

| register | vendor | ours | |
|---|---|---|---|
| `0x0118` | `4a09` | `9055`, then the search walks it to `2323` | gain |
| `0x0122` | `4a09` | never written | a second gain, which we do not set at all |
| `0x0130` | `0346` | `0746` | slot LED selection |
| `0x0132` | `0446` | `0346` | |
| `0x0134` | `0546` | `0246` | |
| `0x0186` | `0406` | `0001` | slot enable |
| `0x0136` | `0000` | `0d20` | LED current - theirs is off |

Two things stand out. They set **two** gain registers to the same value where we set one, so our two
channels have been running at gains that were never matched - which is enough on its own to produce
a ratio that wanders. And they drive **different slots**: our own probe at their gain reports
`desaturated at gain 4a09: ac1=60781 ac2=7092`, sixty thousand counts of pulse against the six we
work with, because at that gain on our slots one channel is railed. It is not the same optical path
overdriven, it is a different path.

The full capture is in `docs/vendor-spo2-registers.txt`.

### What their saturation writes actually do

Eight registers separate their configuration from ours. What each one is, and how strongly:

| register | theirs | ours | what it is | how we know |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `0x0130` | `0346` | `0746` | slot 0 LED selection | measured: changing it changes which channel carries light |
| `0x0132` | `0446` | `0346` | slot 1 LED selection | same |
| `0x0134` | `0546` | `0246` | slot 2 LED selection | same |
| `0x0186` | `0406` | `1807` | slot enable | inferred from position and from the ADT table setting it |
| `0x0136` | `0000` | `0d20` | LED current, per channel | **measured directly** - see the sweep above |
| `0x0080` | `0605` | `0405` | LED drive, per slot | inferred; the vendor's own arrays list it beside 0x0082 |
| `0x0082` | `01c6` | `01c4` | LED drive, per slot | inferred |
| `0x0084` | `0023` | `0123` | integration and drive selection | inferred |

The pattern in `0x0130`-`0x0134` is the readable part. Their three values are `03`, `04`, `05` in the
high byte against a constant `46`; ours are `07`, `03`, `02`. Three slots each pointing at a
different LED, numbered consecutively for them and scattered for us - which is consistent with the
one thing that is beyond doubt, that the two configurations light different emitters.

`0x0136` is the one measured rather than inferred, and it is the surprise: **they turn it off.**
Every experiment in this file assumed more current meant more signal, and the sweep above measured
that it does - level rises linearly with the byte. They run it at zero and get a working
saturation, so on their slots the current comes from `0x0080` and `0x0082` instead.

From Goodix's published example configuration, `gh3011_example_config.h`: **a saturation uses a FIFO
threshold of 100 samples where a heart rate uses 25.** Ours polls the FIFO level rather than setting
a watermark, so this has no equivalent here, but it says their saturation is built on four times as
much data per interrupt as their rate is.

`{0x2A00, 0x00FF}` appears in their saturation array and is worth recording for the next attempt:
`0x2a00` is the value this project once declared "not a register" while truncating the auto-detect
table, on the grounds that the address looked wrong for one.

### What adopting them does

Measured on the same wrist minutes apart, driving our own start sequence and overriding only these
eight registers:

    ours      no rate at all, three runs out of three
    theirs    a rate and a ratio five times out of five
              ac1 28 ac2 32, 18 against 21, 12 against 10 - the two channels within a few counts

So it fixes reliability, which is the failure that loses half our measurements. It does not fix the
saturation: R still centres near 0.97 where their 98% implies about 0.48. Different light, same
disagreement about what the ratio means.

One caution for anyone applying this. Written as a command-line override, after the captured
sequence has finished and committed, it produces the result above. Written into the sequence replay
itself - the obvious place - it does not: three runs, three failures. The registers are the same and
the order is not, so where they land matters and the working placement is the late one.

### On their slots, the ratio repeats

Eight interleaved pairs on one wrist, alternating their eight registers against ours so that
perfusion drift cannot favour either - the method that corrected an earlier wrong conclusion in
this file.

The success rate is not the difference: three of eight for theirs, two of eight for ours, which is
nothing. What differs is what a success looks like.

    theirs, when the amplitude is there    ac1 47 ac2 53   R 0.865
                                           ac1 28 ac2 33   R 0.842
                                           ac1 28 ac2 32   R 0.864
    ours, every success                    ac1  4 ac2  3   R 1.410
                                           ac1  8 ac2  8   R 0.942

**Every large, balanced-channel measurement belongs to their configuration, and those three agree
to within 0.02.** Against a spread of 0.3 to 1.0 for everything else this file records, that is the
first time the ratio has repeated itself at all.

So the instrument is not inherently unstable. It is unstable when the pulse is three counts, which
on our slots it usually is, and steady when it is thirty, which their slots deliver.

That changes what remains. R is 0.85 where their 98% implies about 0.48, and a bias of that size is
a calibration problem - one number, measurable against a fingertip meter, if the ratio holds still
while it is measured. Which it now does, on their slots, when there is signal. Every previous
attempt to calibrate failed because the thing being calibrated moved between measurements.

On by default, and `NOVENDORSLOTS=1` turns it off. Three of eight is thin evidence to change a
measurement path on, and it is on anyway for two reasons: every good measurement of the sixteen
came from it, and the question that remains - how often it clears the amplitude gate over a day
rather than over ten minutes on one afternoon - can only be answered by leaving it running. A day
of readings decides it either way, and the switch reverses it in one word.

### Their ratio is motion-compensated and ours is not - WRONG, see below

From `gh3011_example_process.c`, the call that produces a saturation:

    HBD_Spo2CalculateByFifoIntDbgRawdataInnerUse(
        gsensor_soft_fifo_buffer,        <- the accelerometer fifo
        gsensor_soft_fifo_buffer_index,
        __GS_SENSITIVITY_CONFIG__,
        &spo2_value, &spo2_lvl, &hb_value, &hb_lvl,
        &hrv_val[0..3], &hrv_lvl, &hrv_cnt,
        &spo2_r_value, &wearing_state)

The first three arguments are accelerometer samples, their count, and a sensitivity setting, and
`spo2_r_value` - the ratio itself - comes out of the far end. **Their R is computed from the optical
data and the accelerometer together.** Ours is optical alone.

That is the difference that fits the measurements. Every ratio of 0.6 to 0.8 in these notes was
taken while the watch was worn attentively and still; every 1.7 to 2.8 arrived once the wearer was
asleep and moving in bed. Motion lands in the same band a pulse occupies - an arm swinging at 60 a
minute is a peak at 1 Hz and so is a 60 bpm heart - and nothing in a single optical channel can
separate them. An accelerometer can, which is the whole basis of the published wrist-PPG methods.

ppgd already reads that FIFO, already keeps the magnitudes end to end, and already says in its own
comments that this is why the vendor reads it inside the measurement loop. It then uses them for a
`motion=` figure and a rejection threshold, and never for cleaning the signal the ratio is measured
from.

So the gap is not a register, a calibration or a wrist. It is that we compute a ratio from a
waveform we know contains movement, and reject the result when the movement is obvious rather than
removing it. That is a real piece of work - adaptive cancellation against the accelerometer, or
spectral subtraction of the movement peak before the band amplitudes are taken - and it is the
first candidate that explains a stable 98% on their side and 0.6 to 2.8 on ours without appealing
to anything unmeasured.

| `0x0118` | `6666` | `0000` | gain |
| `0x011a` | `0000` | `004c` | |
| `0x0136` | `0110` | `0404` | LED current |
| `0x012a` | `0303` | `0606` | |
| `0x00c4` | `0528` | `123a` | detector threshold |
| `0x00ce` | `042e` | `1140` | |
| `0x2a16` | `0101` | `0000` | |

**And a block the heart-rate array does not have at all:**

    {0x2000, 0xFFFD}, {0x2001, 0x846C}, {0x2002, 0x0000},
    {0x2003, 0x781D}, {0x2004, 0x000F}, {0x2005, 0xCC34},
    {0x2300, 0x0001}, {0x2301, 0x0001}

Six values pairing into three 32-bit quantities, present for a saturation and absent for a rate,
which is the shape of algorithm coefficients rather than hardware configuration.

**They are not chip registers.** Written through the passthrough they read back as zero, every one.
So the `0x2000` and `0x2a00` ranges are host-side parameters that Goodix's library consumes -
`HBD_LoadNewRegConfigArr` takes the whole array and decides what is a register and what is not.
That retrospectively vindicates the note above calling `0x2a00` "not a register", which was
recorded as an error and was correct.

The consequence is the useful part. A saturation on this part is not only a different optical
configuration, it carries three coefficients the rate mode does not, held on the host. Whatever
they are - and as 32-bit integers they are -162708, 30749 and 1035316, which do not map onto the
110 and -25 of the published curve at any obvious scale - our implementation has nothing
corresponding to them at all. That is a gap in the algorithm rather than in the register writes,
and it is the first explanation for our ratio disagreeing with theirs that does not depend on
anything about wrists or chip state.

Next step is to find where those three are consumed. `hbd_ctrl.h` names the entry points and the
library itself is `libhbd_drv.lib` in the same directory - a static library, so the arithmetic is
in there rather than in the headers.

### Every explanation for the wandering ratio has now been falsified, including the reboot

In order, with the measurement that killed each:

What survives is narrower and physical. `ac2` sits at five to seven counts through all of the above

That has a consequence for everything above it. The calibration `116 - 25R` was fitted to R = 0.713,
measured in the good state; if the good state is "worn well" rather than "recently rebooted", then
the offset is a property of a wrist position and not of this sensor. It needs remeasuring across
several wearings before it means anything.

### The drift is not drift, and the two-pass sequence is the problem

Four things were tried against the ratio wandering, and the answer is none of them.

**Cutting the chip's power does not clear it.** `ioctl(0x40044702, 0)` then `1`, which is what the
vendor's daemon does before every configuration: R read 1.178 before a cycle, 1.133 after, then
2.764. **Nor does the driver's own init**, `0x40044706`: 2.361, 2.697, 2.366 after calling it. Only
a reboot has ever restored the ratio, so whatever accumulates is not in the part's power domain and
not reset by the driver's init entry.

**And it is largely not accumulating at all.** Measured across a single evening rather than
compared against the morning:

    ac1 97  ac2 120   R 0.812
    ac1 24  ac2  41   R 0.585
    ac1 13  ac2   6   R 2.315

That is not a trend, it is two good measurements and a bad one. Every bad reading has channel 2
collapsed below about twelve counts while channel 1 holds up, and R is `(ac1/l1)/(ac2/l2)` - so the
denominator disappearing is the whole of it. Good passes still read 0.6 to 0.8, the same as a
freshly rebooted watch. The "drift" recorded in the previous section was good and bad runs in a
changing mix, not a quantity moving.

`0x0180` looked like the cause - the ratio pass writes it to zero to balance the channels and
nothing put it back - and it is not. Restoring it when ppgd exits did not hold, and not writing it
at all (`NOBALANCE=1`) changed nothing: R stayed at 1.7 to 1.8.

**What does correlate is the two-pass sequence.** Run by hand as a single pass, this sensor gives R
of 0.585, 0.694, 0.812 on the wrist that vitalsd, running the ratio pass and then the long pass,
reports 1.71, 1.82 and 1.83 for. Same code, same minutes, same wrist. Something the ratio pass
leaves behind halves channel 2 for the pass that follows it, and it is not the balance register.

That is the question to answer next, and it is a narrow one: dump the registers between the two
passes and diff them against a single pass's. It also means every ratio this daemon has published
was measured in the contaminated state, including the calibration - `116 - 25R` was fitted to
single-pass measurements and is being applied to two-pass ones.

The saturation is behind `SPO2ABS` because of it. Running unguarded it publishes 70 to 73.

### The saturation, calibrated - and what is still wrong with it

`spo2abs` is published, from `116 - 25R`. The offset is measured rather than assumed: a rebooted
chip gives R = 0.713 across six measurements, the vendor's own curve turns that into 92%, and both
a fingertip meter and their daemon say 98 - so the intercept moves from 110 to 116, and
`116 - 25 * 0.713` is 98.2.

**Only the offset is calibrated.** One anchor fixes where the curve sits and says nothing about how
steep it is, and no desaturation has ever been measured here - breath-holding will not take a
healthy adult below 96. A fall of a few points is worth seeing; its size is the textbook slope's
claim, not this sensor's.

Two averaging bugs were found getting there, and both were producing confidently wrong numbers:

- **The pulse-weighted mean.** The accumulator sums perfusion indices, so one pass with a large
  amplitude and a poor ratio outvotes several good ones. It published 90 and 91 while the passes
  feeding it read 0.729 and 0.748. It publishes the **median** now, which cannot be dragged that
  way, and the same wrist then read 96.
- **Two quantities in one ring.** The ratio pass was adding `rmatch`, about 1.87, and the long pass
  a ratio of about 0.70, to the same buffer. Its median wandered between them as the mix changed -
  `rstable` went 0.700, 1.878, 1.866 across three consecutive requests. One ring, one quantity.

**It is not finished.** After those fixes the ring reads 1.83 to 1.93 on the same wrist, where the
same code an hour earlier read 0.70 - the degradation described in the section above, returning.
At that ratio the curve gives 68%, which is below the floor, so nothing is published rather than
something alarming. That is the guard working, not the measurement working.

So: the calibration is sound and measured, the averaging is now honest, and the input still moves
by a factor of two and a half over an evening. The next thing to fix is the drift, and the reboot
result says it is chip state rather than physiology.

### A reboot fixes the ratio, and that explains the whole day

Six measurements on a freshly rebooted watch, our own configuration, nothing else changed:

    ac1  90  ac2 131   R 0.694        ac1 43  ac2 58   R 0.739
    ac1  54  ac2  77   R 0.706        ac1 84  ac2 106  R 0.787
    ac1   9  ac2  13   R 0.668        ac1 25  ac2  37  R 0.682

**R of 0.713 with a spread of 0.12**, against 0.3 to 1.9 across the same afternoon before the

same wrist and the same code gave 3.

So the instrument is repeatable, and what makes it not repeatable is accumulated chip state. A day
of register sweeps, frozen gains, interrupted probes and killed processes leaves it degraded, and
nothing short of a reboot clears it. Every conclusion in this file drawn from measurements that
disagreed with each other minutes apart is suspect for that reason, including several already
marked as corrections.

That also retires the factor of two recorded above. It rested on R of 0.906 and 0.976, measured on
a chip in exactly that degraded state; on a clean one R is 0.71 and halving it gives 105%, which is
not a saturation. Two measurements landing on a reference was a coincidence, and a third would have
settled it - which is the pattern this file keeps recording and this session kept repeating.

What is left is a real calibration problem, stated properly for the first time: R is 0.713 and
steady, their curve turns that into 92%, and both the meter and their own daemon say 98. A gap of
six points against a ratio that now holds still to a tenth is a single number to find, and finding
it needs a clean chip and a meter - not more register archaeology.

**Practical consequence for the daemon.** If measurements start failing or the ratio starts
wandering, reboot before believing anything they say. That is now the first thing to try rather
than the last.

### Our ratio is twice theirs, and halving it lands on the meter

Two clean measurements on our own configuration, good amplitude, still wrist:

    ac1 30  ac2 33   R 0.906      110 - 25*(R/2) = 98.7%
    ac1 24  ac2 25   R 0.976      110 - 25*(R/2) = 97.8%

The fingertip meter read 98 to 99 at the time, and their daemon reported 98 on the same wrist

**It is not explained.** Both channels go through the same band_amp and the same pedestal
arithmetic, so a constant factor cancels in a ratio of ratios - which means the two cannot be the
same measurement differing by a scale, and one of these is wrong about what it is measuring. Two
candidates, neither tested:

- the coefficients. `FUN_0001f8c0` fills 0.0, -25.0 and 110.0 as defaults when a flag is clear, and
  this file records that nothing overwrites them. That was read from the disassembly, not watched
  at runtime, and a configuration loaded later would not have been seen.
- the channel assignment. If their numerator is our denominator the curve is being fed the
  reciprocal, though 1/0.94 does not land anywhere near 98% either, so this needs the actual
  definition rather than an assumption about it.

Recorded because two independent measurements landing within a point of a reference is worth
chasing, and because the temptation is to divide by two and publish - which is the same mistake as
the 86% this file already documents, made with more arithmetic in front of it.

### How they actually read it

The i2c tap was attached to their daemon and a measurement triggered. It answers the question the
register dump could not, because what they do differently is mostly not registers.

    IOCTL 00004701   wait for the interrupt      blocks, and returns when data is ready
    R 0008 = 0002    status: new data
    IOCTL 825a470a   read 602 bytes of samples   _IOR('G', 0x0a, 602)
    R 0122           the applied gain, read back - their AGC moves it, 9055 -> 4f55
    W dd dd c3       arm the next read
    IOCTL 40184709   publish the result

Three things in that loop we do not do at all.

**They wait on the interrupt and it works.** Ours returns success immediately without an interrupt,
which is why the measurement path polls the FIFO instead - documented above as a gap with no known
cause. Theirs blocks properly, so whatever arms it is in the startup below rather than absent from
the hardware.

**They read samples through the driver, not over the passthrough.** `0x825a470a` returns 602 bytes
in one call. We read the FIFO register in three-byte samples over i2c, on a bus that returns the
previous value often enough to have invented three separate conclusions in this file.

**There is a command we did not know about.** `W dd dd a1`, issued once between the start and the
first sample read. Our set was c0, c1, c3, c4.

`docs/vendor-spo2-writes.txt` has all 47 writes, `docs/vendor-spo2-ioctls.txt` the call sequence
around them. The differences from what our own sequence leaves:

Applying it. An override of `0118` and `0122` does not survive - the start sequence writes the gain

### Their daemon measures a saturation, and the difference is one register

Everything above this point reasoned about the vendor's saturation from register dumps, from their
binary, and from a curve fitted to a finger meter. Their daemon has now been made to measure one,
on this wrist, while its traffic was recorded, so the comparison is measured rather than argued.

**Nothing on this watch has ever asked for a saturation.** The daemon takes its orders through
`/dev/gh_tools`, not through a socket: it blocks in `gh_dev_wait_cmd` on `_IORW('G', 8, 4)` and the
sensor HAL sends commands with `_IOW('G', 9, 24)`, a 24-byte packet whose first word is the command.
The handler accepts 4, 5 and 7 and nothing else. 4 is heart rate, 7 is the LED factory self-test,
and 5 is `RUN_MODE_SPO2_DET` - behind a flag which is zero here, so `HeartRateSensor::setEnable`
never sends it. That is why triggering through `com.ic.work` produced an idle daemon and no error.

`tools/gh3011/ghcmd.c` sends the packet directly. `ghcmd 5` starts a saturation, `ghcmd 6` stops it.

With the watch worn, their daemon reports:

    spo2 calc, gs_len=40, result=97,72,74,0,660,690,0,0,40,2,0,1
    GH_3011 spo2_result : 97 , lvl 71 , hb 0, lvl0 , wearing 1

**97%, level 71, and a heart rate of 74, held steady over a fifty-second run.** The eleventh result
is `spo2_r_value` and this build leaves it zero, so their ratio is not printed - but their curve is
now known exactly, and 110 - 25R = 97 puts their R at **0.52**.

Ours, on the same wrist a minute later, measured pulse amplitudes of 4.3 and 5.2 counts and declined
to produce a ratio at all.

That is the whole diff. Fifty-seven registers identical, including every one this file previously

#### What that costs us, and what is not yet established

Their saturation mode will not accept a pulse amplitude below thirty-four counts. Four consecutive
runs of ours on the same sleeping wrist, minutes after their daemon had been reporting 97% steadily,
gave amplitudes of 5.0 and 5.9, 6.7 and 8.7, 4.6 and 5.8, 7.1 and 8.1 - and `no_agreement` every
time. A ratio of two six-count numbers is quantisation noise, and that - not the wrist, not sleep,
not perfusion, not the chip's power state - is what the ratio of 0.6 to 2.8 has been all along.

What is **not** established is that copying `0x4f44` fixes it. Pinned there, our channel 2 rails
immediately: level 64,852, amplitude 0.1. So does every other value tried, from `0x1c1c` to
`0x5620`, and once railed the part stays railed into the next run - a plain run afterwards clears
it. A value that looked good was simply the first run after the daemon had left the part in a good
state, and it railed too when tried again later. So `0x0118` is not a plain analogue gain, our model
of it is wrong, and the sweep says so rather than the sweep being trusted.

What our gain loop *is* doing is now clear regardless: at `0x2828` channel 2 rails, the loop's
`hi_lvl > 48000` rule backs it off, one step of an eighth drops the level from 64,852 to 19,801, and
it stops there with a four-count pulse. The window between railed and starved is one step wide with
a single shared gain. The vendor's is not one gain, and that is the next thing to chase.

The loop is also path-dependent, which was not known. Started by hand at different gains and then
left to run, it does not converge on one place:

    from 0x2828   ->  0x2323    levels 20,617 / 19,799    amplitudes 15.9 / 16.2
    from 0x4f44   ->  0x351b    levels 15,112 / 30,663    amplitudes  6.5 / 17.7
    from 0x1818   ->  0x1202    no samples at all

So where it settles is a property of where it started, and the start is `0x2828` only because this
file put it there. That is worth remembering next to the older note wondering why the same `0x0084`
setting finished at 4,500 in one session and 50,364 in another.

One more thing the sweep settles: the level does not follow the value. Frozen at `0x1818`, `0x2020`,
`0x2828`, `0x4f44` and `0x5620`, every run reports the same dc1 near 3,202,030 and the same railed
dc2 of 3,210,580, while a plain run between any two of them recovers completely. Whatever moves the
level, writing `0x0118` on its own before sampling is not it - and the loop's own note that a gain
change steps the DC by about 9,500 counts cannot reach the 45,000 that separates railed from
settled, so that note and this are describing different mechanisms.

### What the waveform actually contains, measured directly

After flip-flopping twice on which measurement regime was the clean one, the raw samples were dumped
in both and analysed outside `ppgd` - no beat detection, no baseline interpolation, just a Goertzel
at chosen frequencies over 26 seconds of settled samples.

    capture     dominant component        pulse at the measured rate
    adaptive    41.5 bpm, amp 195/188     60 bpm, amp 61.0 / 40.9
    pinned      48-51 bpm, amp 261/247    59 bpm, amp 17.7 / 22.7

**The waveform is dominated by something below the heart rate, three to ten times larger than the
pulse.** Whatever it is - baseline wander, a vasomotor rhythm, the arm settling - it is common to
both channels to within a few percent, and a peak-to-baseline amplitude measured across it is mostly
measuring it rather than the pulse. Two channels seeing the same large common signal give a ratio
near one, which is what this file has been recording and explaining for most of a day.

Taken at the heart rate instead, the ratio is **1.504** in one capture and **0.782** in the other,
minutes apart on a still wrist. That is not a measurement, and it says the pulse is too weak to
carry a ratio: 18 to 61 counts, against the 34 their own saturation mode sets as its floor.

So the gain work helped and did not go far enough, and the conclusion is the one this file reached
in the morning by a different route. The problem is amplitude. Everything downstream - the factor of
two, the divisor, the curve - is arithmetic on a pulse that is not yet big enough to divide.

`rband` deserves a note here too. It was added to test exactly this and reported agreement with the
beat-wise figure to three decimals, which was taken as evidence the amplitude was pulse. Against the
Goertzel it is not: `nb1` tracks `ac1` because both are dominated by the same low-frequency
component, not because either is measuring a heartbeat. A test that agrees with the thing it is
checking has not checked it.

### The official header, and what 0x0118 actually is

Goodix's own `hbd_ctrl.h` and reference arrays are public in a third-party repository and are now
kept under `docs/data/`. They settle several things this file had been guessing at, and one of them
overturns a name used throughout.

**`0x0118` is not the gain. It is the two logic channels' LED currents.** From the documented
default init config:

    0x19, 0x19,                    /// Logic Channel0/1 current 10mA (1 step = 400uA)
    HBD_TIA_GAIN_3, HBD_TIA_GAIN_3,
    HBD_SAMPLE_TIME_128CYCLES

`0x28, 0x28` in the reference array is exactly the `0x2828` this file has been writing to `0x0118`
and calling a gain. One byte per logic channel, 400 µA a step - which is why one code step moved the
DC by about a quarter, why the two bytes behave per-channel, and why "the level does not follow the
gain" was so hard to make sense of. Every note here about the gain loop is really about the LED
current loop. The TIA gain is a separate field with six settings and has never been touched.

**There are exactly two logic channels, not a slot-enable question.** The init config has
`emLogicChannel0PDSelect` and `emLogicChannel1PDSelect` and nothing further, and the three physical
LEDs are permuted onto them by `emLedLogicChannelMap` - `PHY012`, `PHY021`, `PHY102` and so on. So
the FIFO carries two channels because the library has two, and which two wavelengths arrive is a
choice of that permutation. `0x0186 = 0x0407` was tried on the theory that a third slot could be
enabled and the stream stayed at period 2, which is consistent: there is no third channel to enable.

The reference reads `HBD_LED_LOGIC_CHANNEL_MAP_PHY012`, which with the datasheet's LED assignment -
DRV0 infrared, DRV1 red, DRV2 green - puts infrared on logic 0 and red on logic 1. That is the right
pair for a saturation.

#### The reference array against this watch, and which difference matters

    reg       reference   this watch
    0x0118     0x6666      0x9055     LED currents
    0x0130     0x1546      0x0346
    0x0132     0x1746      0x0446
    0x0134     0x0546      0x0546     same
    0x0136     0x0110      0x0000
    0x0180     0x008d      0x004d
    0x0186     0x0406      0x0406     same

Applying all of them together gives R = 1.004 and 1.016 - worse than `0x0136` alone. Isolating:

    0x0136=0110                    ac1 105  ac2 250   R 0.451
    0x0136=0110, 0x0180=008d       ac1  87  ac2  86   R 0.954     <- undoes it
    0x0136=0110, 0x0130=1546       ac1 110  ac2 253   R 0.468
    0x0136=0110, 0x0132=1746       ac1 112  ac2 254   R 0.475

**`0x0180` is the one that must keep this board's value.** The reference's `0x008d` collapses the
channel balance back to 1.0; `0x004d` is right here. This file already had `0x0180` noted as
affecting channel balance, from the two-pass work, and this is the same register biting again.

So the minimal change is `0x0136 = 0x0110` on its own, and the reference values for `0x0130` and
`0x0132` are harmless either way.

#### A candidate for the bimodality

The tracking run's amplitudes were bimodal - `ac1` near 170 or near 105 with nothing between - which
is the shape of a discrete setting changing rather than a physiological quantity. The TIA gain is a
discrete setting, six values, sitting in the init config next to the LED currents, never read and
never written by this code. If the part or the library moves it between two values while running,
that is exactly what the two states would look like. It is the first hypothesis for the bimodality
that predicts discreteness rather than explaining it away, and it is testable by reading the TIA
gain register across passes.

So tracking is not established, for the third time today, and this run says something more specific
than the last two: at fixed gain and fixed DC, both amplitudes move together by a factor of 1.6 and
their ratio moves from 1.75 to 2.2. That is a perfusion-linked change in the amplitude ratio, which
is a known confounder in wrist PPG and is larger here than the saturation signal it would have to
sit under.

#### What the day actually established

The register finding is real and worth keeping, and it is not the same claim as a working
saturation:

* `0x0136` was zero in the shipped configuration where Goodix reference sets `0x0110`
* with it set, channel 2 carries about twice channel 1 rather than the same, which is the first time
  the two channels have looked like a wavelength pair at all
* R moves into 0.48 to 0.63 from a pinned 1.0, which is the physiological range
* at rest it is repeatable to 0.015 across consecutive passes

None of that is a measurement of saturation, and the honest reading is that this is now a better
signal that still is not one. The remaining obstacle is no longer the channel balance or the scale;
it is that the amplitude ratio has a second, larger dependence on perfusion, and separating the two
needs either a reference that varies independently of perfusion or the vendor own algorithm, which
by its own flat 98 does not solve it either.

### 0x0136 was zero, and that is why there was no ratio

The section below concluded that this sensor configuration cannot measure saturation, and that the
two channels were not a usable red/infrared pair. The first half was right about the *configuration*
and wrong to read it as the hardware. The wearer pointed at the datasheet, which says plainly that
the part measures SpO2, and then looked at the back of the watch: **red light is visible.** So a red
LED is fitted and the pair exists.

What was wrong is one register. Comparing this watch's saturation array against Goodix's published
reference array:

    register          Goodix reference     this watch
    0x0136                 0x0110            0x0000       <- LED drive
    0x0130                 0x1546            0x0346

`0x0136` is the register this file already identified as the LED drive, and had noted with the
remark that "theirs is off entirely" - recorded as a curiosity rather than chased. Setting it to the
reference value, alternated against the shipped value on one wrist:

    0x0136     ac1              ac2              ac2/ac1   R
    0x0000     85, 134, 84      83, 125, 82       ~1.0     0.964, 1.009, 0.970
    0x0110     103, 105, 101    227, 228, 227      2.2     0.482, 0.486, 0.471

A ratio spread of 0.015 across three runs, and channel 2 carrying 2.2 times channel 1 - which is
what a genuine two-wavelength pair looks like, and what near-identical perfusion indices were the
absence of.

**And it lands on their scale.** Their curve, applied directly with no divisor at all:

    R = 0.471  ->  98.8%
    R = 0.480  ->  98.5%        our mean
    R = 0.486  ->  98.3%
    R = 0.496  ->  98.0%        theirs, for the 98% it reports

The meter read 97 to 99 throughout. So the divisor that was fitted at 2, then argued up to 3.6, is
**1.0** - because with the LED driven properly our ratio is their ratio, and the fitting was always
compensating for a register.

**This also explains their flat 98.** Their daemon runs with `0x0136 = 0x0000` too - it is in the
wire capture of their own saturation run. Their shipped configuration has the same defect as ours,
their ratio is therefore pinned near 1 as ours was, and their curve at R = 1.0 gives 81.8%, which
their algorithm evidently floors or discards into a constant. That is very likely why saturation is
switched off behind a flag on this product: not because the sensor cannot do it, but because the
configuration they shipped cannot.

What is **not** yet shown is that it tracks. A ratio on the right scale with the right channel
balance is necessary and not sufficient, and the only test that matters is a desaturation. That is
the next measurement, and for the first time it is worth running.

### Their saturation does not track either - but the cause is one register, not the hardware

The question nobody had asked: every vendor reading on record - 97, 98, 99 - was taken at rest, and
a plausible constant looks exactly like that. So their daemon was run through a breath hold with a
finger meter, its output sampled every ten seconds. It reports about once a second once converged,
which is far finer than our thirty-second passes, so the wearer's timing barely matters.

    the meter        97 -> 90 -> 98      a seven point desaturation
    their daemon     98 throughout

Over the whole run, **189 of their 196 non-zero reports were exactly 98**. The other seven were 95
and 96 during the first fifteen seconds while it converged. Their own confidence figure sat between
42 and 76 the whole time - it never flagged the reading as poor.

So the vendor's saturation is a plausible constant on this hardware, exactly as ours is. Ours sat
between 96 and 98 while the meter went 99 to 91; theirs sat at 98 while the meter went 97 to 90.
Neither measures saturation.

**And this explains something already in this file.** Command 5, `RUN_MODE_SPO2_DET`, is behind a
flag that is zero on this watch, and `HeartRateSensor::setEnable` in the sensor HAL only ever sends
4, 5 and 6 - so nothing on this product has ever asked the daemon for a saturation. That was
recorded as an obstacle to work around, and it is better read as a decision: the vendor shipped this
sensor configuration with saturation switched off, and the measurement above is why.

#### What that closes

Everything about SpO2 on this watch, and the closing is worth being explicit about because a great
deal of work went into the parts that were real:

* their curve is exact, read out of the binary and confirmed against their reported values
* their configuration is captured to the register
* their motion handling is mapped end to end
* their gain levels are known and adopting them roughly doubled our pulse amplitude
* our gain loop's hunting was real, and fixing it was worth doing for the heart rate too

None of it produces a saturation, because the two channels reaching the FIFO are not a usable
red/infrared pair. Their perfusion indices differ by 1.14 with a standard deviation of 0.14 across
five-second windows, where a genuine pair at 98% differs by about two. A ratio of two channels
carrying the same information is one, plus noise, forever - which is what both implementations
report.

The remaining hypothesis is that the useful channel is the third slot. Their configuration drives
LEDs 3, 4 and 5 at `0x0130`, `0x0132` and `0x0134`, `0x0186` enables `0x0406`, and only two channels
reach the FIFO. If saturation on this part needs a slot that is not enabled here, that is a
configuration question with a definite answer - but it is a different project from calibrating a
ratio, and the ratio work should stop rather than continue.

### Run: our formula cannot produce their answer from their data

Their daemon configured the part, measured, and reported **98%**. It was then frozen with SIGSTOP -
leaving its fd open, so the driver did not halt the part - and 30 seconds of the stream it left
behind was read with `fifograb`: 6200 samples in 31 reads, exactly 200 a second, two channels at a
hundred hertz. Their configuration, their gain, their FIFO.

Our formula on those samples gives:

    ch1  level 46,823   amp@55.5bpm 40.12   perfusion 0.00086
    ch2  level 47,668   amp@55.5bpm 34.13   perfusion 0.00072
    R = 1.197

Their curve puts 98% at R = 0.496. So **the configuration is not the difference** - the answer the
experiment was built to give - but the conclusion is stronger than expected, because 1.197 is not a
mis-tuned 0.496. It cannot be reached at all:

    R = (A1/L1) / (A2/L2) = (A1/A2) x (L2/L1)

Channel 2 has the **higher** DC and the **lower** amplitude at the pulse. So L2/L1 is above one for
every pedestal below the DC, A1/A2 is 1.176, and the product is therefore above 1.176 whatever
pedestal is chosen. No pedestal reaches 0.496. Nor does swapping the channels, which gives 0.835.
Nor does choosing another frequency: across the band the amplitude ratio runs from 0.41 to 6.27 and
passes through 0.496 only where both amplitudes are under two counts and mean nothing.

The interleave was checked on their stream rather than assumed, since ours had only ever been
checked on ours: mean absolute difference is 3.2 at lag 2 against 902 at lags 1 and 3, so it is two
channels and the de-interleaving is right.

**So the quantity we compute is not the quantity they compute.** Not a calibration, not a
configuration, not a pedestal, not a scale factor - a different definition. Everything this file has
done to tune R has been tuning the wrong number, which is why 116 became 110 became a divisor of two
became a divisor of 3.6 and none of them held.

What their `spo2_r_value` actually is remains open, and it is now the only question that matters.
The obvious candidates - a per-beat ratio with shape weighting, a normalisation by LED current, an
ambient or dark slot subtracted before the ratio is formed - are all testable against this same
captured stream, which is the useful thing about having it. Their data and their answer are both on
disk now, so a candidate definition either reproduces 0.496 from these samples or it does not.

### Built and not yet run: their configuration, our reader

The calibration says the ratio does not respond, and no curve repairs that. But their daemon gets a
working saturation from this sensor, these registers and this FIFO, so the information is in the
hardware and the extraction is losing it. The next measurement splits those two.

    tools/gh3011/theirconfig.sh    their daemon configures and runs a saturation, reports its answer,
                                   is killed rather than stopped so the part keeps streaming, and
    tools/gh3011/fifograb.c        drains the FIFO with their settings still in place, configuring
                                   nothing, and
    tools/gh3011/stream-ratio.py   computes R from that stream narrowband, off the raw samples

Then it splits cleanly:

    R from their stream still near 1.05   ->  the fault is our arithmetic, and it is findable
    R from their stream near 0.5          ->  the fault is our configuration, and theirs is the fix

Killing rather than stopping is the part that matters. `setprop ctl.stop` lets the daemon run its
shutdown, which halts the part and leaves nothing to read; SIGKILL leaves it exactly as their code
last set it. Reading *alongside* their daemon was the first design and is worse - two processes
draining one hardware FIFO each get a decimated stream full of gaps, and a one hertz pulse cannot be
measured in the quarter-second bursts that survive.

`stream-ratio.py` is deliberately a second opinion rather than a reimplementation. `ppgd` measures
peak-to-baseline beat by beat, and that method has twice been dominated by something other than the
pulse - DC steps from the hunting loop, then a sub-cardiac component three to ten times larger than
the heartbeat - and produced a steady, plausible, wrong ratio both times. Narrowband at one
frequency excludes both by construction rather than by a threshold.

Everything is built, deployed and verified to start; the measurement itself is waiting on a wearer.

### The calibration run, on the fixed loop: it does not track

Sixteen passes over eight and a half minutes against a finger meter, four voluntary breath holds,
the wearer still throughout - `gssd` between 5 and 18 where the quiet band starts near 47 - and the
gain loop settling properly at 0x3b3b to 0x3d3d with amplitudes of 80 to 120 counts, comfortably
over the vendor's floor of 34. Everything that was wrong with the first attempt was fixed.

The meter went from **99 down to 91** across the four holds. Ours:

    meter over the run    91 to 99      8 points
    our published spo2    96 to 98      2 points
    our R                 0.969 to 1.114

For our ratio to have tracked that swing it had to move 0.523. It moved 0.145 - **28 percent of it**
- and that is an upper bound, because some of the 0.145 is pass-to-pass noise rather than signal.
The standard deviation of R across all sixteen passes is 0.038, and across the five stillest 0.026,
so a good part of the range is scatter.

This conclusion needs no timing at all, which is the point of running it this way. It does not
matter which pass sat in which dip: the meter moved eight points, the ratio had four clear
opportunities to follow it, and the published figure sat between 96 and 98 for the whole run.

So the ratio does not respond to a real desaturation on this hardware, and no divisor or curve fixes
that. A calibration maps one number onto another; it cannot make a number that does not move start
moving. Everything upstream of the curve - the amplitude, the two channels having near-identical
pulsatility, the pulse being a fraction of the baseline wander it sits on - has to be right first,
and the direct waveform measurement above says it is not.

What this does settle, usefully, is that the published saturation is not a measurement of
saturation. It is a roughly constant 97 with noise on it, and it happens to sit near this wearer's
resting value because the divisor was fitted to make it do so.

### The gain loop was hunting, and the ratio was measuring it

The split by settled gain turned out not to be about gain, and chasing it found something worse
underneath.

**Holding the gain equal kills the split.** `GAINFIX=<code>` lets the loop work for six seconds -
long enough to bring the part off the rail, which is why `FREEZEGAIN` could never do this - and then
pins it. Ten passes alternating the two codes on one wrist:

    0x3939   n=5   R mean 1.786  sd 0.053
    0x3a3a   n=5   R mean 1.816  sd 0.037
    difference 0.030, against 0.092 when the loop chose, and the sign reversed

So the gain was standing in for something else.

**What it stood in for is the loop never settling.** Their 40000 is a floor and this wrist sits at
40,772 - a few hundred counts the wrong side of a threshold the loop used as a trigger rather than a
target. One code is worth about a quarter of the level, so it stepped up, overshot, drifted back,
and stepped again for as long as the pass lasted. Every change restarts the analysis window, so a
thirty-second pass ended with nothing settled in it. Three adaptive passes in a row reported
`dc1=0` while three pinned passes interleaved between them read normally.

**And that is why R was ever near 1.0.** A gain change puts a DC step of about 9,500 counts into
both channels at once - two orders above the pulse, and common to the pair. An amplitude ratio
measured across such a step is driven towards one. With the gain held still:

    pinned    ac1 24-31    ac2 13-17    ac1/ac2 ~1.85   R 1.78 - 1.94
    hunting   ac1 95-142   ac2 81-126   ac1/ac2 ~1.03   R ~1.0

The amplitudes differ fourfold and the ratio by a factor of nearly two, on the same wrist within the
hour. The hunting figures are the wrong ones: inflated by the steps and equalised by them.

A deadband fixes it - move only when the level is genuinely outside the band, stop once inside -
with a hard cap of twelve changes per pass, so that however the band is drawn a pass can never end
with nothing analysable in it. With that, an adaptive thirty-second pass completes and reads
`r=1.790` against the pinned `1.78-1.94`. The two regimes agree.

**What this costs.** Everything fitted to the old ratio is void: the factor of two against their
daemon, both paired comparisons, and the divisor in `spo2v`. The settled ratio against their 0.496
implies a divisor near 3.6 rather than 2.0, and that is not adopted either, because their reading
came through the same broken loop. The comparison has to be run again.

Worth saying plainly: this is the second time in this file a ratio has been steady, plausible,
repeatable and an artefact of the code measuring it. The first was two counts divided by itself.
Repeatability was treated as evidence both times, and it is evidence of nothing except that the same
thing happens every run.

### A breath-hold run, and the artefact it exposed

Fourteen passes over five and a half minutes against a finger meter, with two voluntary breath holds.
The meter went 97 down to 94 and back to 98, then 98 down to 94 and back to 99. The wearer was very
still throughout - `gssd` between 5 and 10, where the quiet band from the accelerometer log starts at
about 47 - so movement is not in this.

Two things came out and only one of them is about saturation.

**The magnitude is right.** Our ratio spanned 0.945 to 1.230 over the run, a range of 0.285. A four
point swing in saturation is 0.131 in their ratio and 0.262 in ours at the divisor of two. So the
ratio moves by about as much as a real desaturation should move it.

**But it also splits by the gain the loop happens to settle on**, and that is not something a ratio
of ratios is allowed to do:

    gain 0x3939   n=5   R mean 1.132    ac1/ac2 1.122    level1 36245  level2 36570
    gain 0x3a3a   n=9   R mean 1.040    ac1/ac2 1.029    level1 36237  level2 36631

0.092 between them, a third of the whole physiological swing. The pedestal does not explain it -
sweeping it from 2.5 to 3.0 units leaves the split at 0.093 throughout - and the DC levels are the
same to a few hundred counts in both groups, so it is not an operating-point effect either. The
entire difference lives in `ac1/ac2`, the amplitude ratio, at identical light levels.

Which leaves it unresolved rather than diagnosed. The gain may be causing it, or the gain may merely
be tracking whatever else is: the loop chooses a code from the level it sees while converging, so a
run that arrives at 0x3939 got there by a different path than one that arrives at 0x3a3a. Fourteen
points cannot separate those.

**The run does not calibrate anything, and the reason is the clock.** The meter readings were called
out live and arrive here as messages; what is knowable is when each was *received*, not when it was
read, and the batching puts that uncertainty at ten to twenty seconds against a dip that is
comparable in width. Correlating passes against meter values on reconstructed timestamps would be
fitting a curve to invented times, so it was not done.

What that argues for next is a protocol that does not depend on message timing at all: several holds
alternating with rest over a quarter of an hour, comparing pooled ratios from stretches that are
unambiguously at rest against stretches that are unambiguously in a dip. That averages over both the
timing slop and the gain split, and it is the same amount of the wearer's attention spent better.

### The factor of two, measured against their daemon twice

Their daemon and ours were run on one wrist minutes apart, theirs driven into saturation mode by
hand. Their curve is known exactly - the host parameter block above - so their percentage inverts
to their ratio and the two can be put side by side:

    theirs 98%   ->  their R 0.4959    ours 1.0490 over three runs    ratio 2.115
    theirs 97%   ->  their R 0.5275    ours 0.9907 over three runs    ratio 1.878

Two is inside both. Put through their curve at half, our ratios give 97.1% where they said 98 and
98.0% where they said 97: a point out either way, in opposite directions, which is the twelve
percent between the two ratios appearing as two points of saturation.

That is enough to compute and log, and nowhere near enough to publish. A saturation curve is only
tested by a saturation that moves, and neither comparison had one - both wearer and room were the
same, a single percent apart. `spo2v` in the output is their curve applied to half our ratio, and
on a still wrist it reads

    r=1.050 gssd=96  spo2v=97.1
    r=1.005 gssd=89  spo2v=97.8
    r=1.020 gssd=86  spo2v=97.6

against their 98 on the same arm. It is a number to watch across a night, not one to show anyone.
This file published a confident 86% once, off a ratio that was two counts divided by itself, and the
note that followed warned against exactly this arithmetic. What has changed is that the ratio is now
repeatable to a couple of percent and their number is on the table beside it - which is why it is
computed, not why it should be believed.

What has *not* been explained is why the factor is two. Both channels carry the same pulsatility to
within a few percent, run after run, on a sensor where their own algorithm gets a ratio near a half
from the same registers and the same FIFO. A definitional difference in how they form R would
account for it and cannot be shown from here; so would a channel of ours being something other than
what it is taken for, and the tests that would separate those need a still wrist and are the next
thing to do rather than the next thing to guess.

### Chasing the factor of two: two explanations killed, none found

Their R is 0.527 and ours is about 1.0 on the same wrist. Two candidates were tested and both are
dead, which is worth recording because both were plausible enough to have been written up as the
answer.

**The channel pairing is right.** `ppgd` reads the FIFO six bytes at a time and calls them two
channels, and this file has doubted that in writing - the vendor drives three slots, and reading a
three-slot stream two at a time would explain a great deal. `RAWDUMP` settles it. Over 3,166 raw
samples the mean absolute difference between samples one, two and three apart is

    period 1   461.0
    period 2    44.8      <- the stream
    period 3   496.3

so the stream is two interleaved channels and the pairing is correct.

**The amplitude is the pulse, not common mode.** Both channels see the same wrist and the same arm,
so anything common divides out to one, and a ratio pinned near 1.0 is what a measurement dominated
by common mode looks like from outside. Measured against a narrow band at the rate that was actually
found - `nb1`, `nb2` and `rband` in the output - the two agree exactly:

    ac1=89  ac2=89   r=1.006    nb1=89.4  nb2=88.6   rband=1.006
    ac1=89  ac2=92   r=0.969    nb1=89.3  nb2=91.8   rband=0.969
    ac1=80  ac2=83   r=0.959    nb1=80.2  nb2=83.3   rband=0.959
    ac1=163 ac2=168  r=0.967    nb1=162.7 nb2=167.8  rband=0.967

Three decimal places on four runs. The broadband figure is not picking up drift or movement; the two
channels really do have the same pulsatility to within a few percent, run after run.

Which is the odd part, and where this stops. Red and infrared through tissue are not expected to
agree that closely, and R near 1.0 is where the standard curves put a saturation in the mid-eighties
rather than the 97% their daemon reads off the same sensor minutes earlier. Registers `0x011c`,
`0x011e` and `0x0120` were tried as per-slot currents and are not - zeroing each in turn leaves both
channels lit - and tests on the slot LED select at `0x0130`/`0x0132`/`0x0134` were run while the
wearer was up and moving, so the one suggestive result out of four is not worth reporting as
anything. That needs a still wrist and a run against their daemon on the same arm, which is the next
measurement rather than the next idea.

### The host parameters, read out of the daemon

Everything past register `0x1fff` is a library parameter rather than a chip register. That was
guessed at earlier from readbacks returning zero; it is now exact. `FUN_00017198` walks the
configuration array the daemon hands the library, and splits it on one constant:

    id  > 0x1fff  ->  FUN_000151e8, a host-side parameter setter
    id <= 0x1fff  ->  written to the part over i2c, read back and verified

`DAT_00017208` holds that constant and it is `0x1fff`.

The array itself was found by its contents rather than by chasing pointers - two attempts at the
GOT arithmetic produced a "table" that decoded as Thumb code and another that pointed into the
string section, so the wire capture was used as a signature instead. Entries 23 to 69 match the
saturation writes recorded on the bus exactly, which is what makes the rest of it trustworthy.

Past the registers, this watch's saturation configuration carries:

    0x2030..0x2035    three 32-bit values   -10223, -305835, 1134171
    0x2a00            0x00ff
    0x2a02..0x2a15    ten 32-bit values     60000 38000 58000 40000 10000
                                            60000 38000 58000 40000 14000
    0x2a16, 0x2a17    0x0101, 0x0602
    0x2b03            1
    0x2b07, 0x2b0c    20, 20
    0x2b0f            30

**The first three are the saturation curve for this watch.** They are the override for the
coefficients whose defaults - 0.0, -25.0 and 110.0 - the initialiser at `0x1f8c0` writes, and at a
scale of ten thousand they give

    SpO2 = -1.0223 R^2 - 30.5835 R + 113.4171

Their daemon reported 97% on this wrist, and inverting that curve puts their R at **0.527**. The
default line would have put it at 0.52, so the two agree about where their ratio sits, and the
number stands whichever curve is used.

**The ten after them are the gain control's levels**, five per channel, in counts above the
pedestal. Read against what the loop in `ppgd.c` was doing:

    60000   the rail to hurry away from
    58000   ceiling
    40000   floor for raising
    38000   target
    10000 / 14000   the two channels' minimum usable levels

The band this file had guessed - 0.55 and 0.88 of a 64,852 span, or 35,669 and 57,070 - was close
enough to work and low enough to settle around 32,500, under their floor. Using their numbers takes
the amplitudes from 75 counts to between 96 and 447 on the same wrist, with the ratio holding
between 0.94 and 1.17 through movement that a few hours earlier would have produced nothing at all.

The parameter setter is two switches, biased by `-0x2A00` and `-0x2B00`, so a case number is the
id's low byte. Cases 2, 4, 6 ... 0x14 of the first share one handler - ids `0x2A02` to `0x2A14`,
the ten gain levels above, which confirms that block from the other direction.

**The classifier's thresholds are ids `0x2A36` to `0x2A3B`**: six consecutive cases writing six
consecutive offsets through the same base pointer, the one `FUN_00017820` reads its five thresholds
from. This watch's array does not contain them. It sets `0x2030`-`0x2035`, `0x2a00`, `0x2a02`-
`0x2a17`, and `0x2b03`, `0x2b07`, `0x2b0c`, `0x2b0f` - and nothing in the `0x2A36` range - so the
library's own defaults are what classify movement here.

That also retires a guess made a few paragraphs ago in an earlier pass: `0x2b07`, `0x2b0c` and
`0x2b0f` at 20, 20 and 30 are *not* the motion thresholds. They are cases 7, 0xc and 0xf of the
second switch, and each writes its own separate global.

### What the vendor actually does about movement: nothing to the waveform

The section above, and the commit that added it, said their ratio is motion-compensated and ours is
not. That was inferred from a function signature - `HBD_Spo2CalculateByFifoIntDbgRawdataInnerUse`
takes the g-sensor FIFO as its first argument - and the inference was wrong. The signature is real.
What they do with the buffer is not compensation.

Followed through the binary, the accelerometer enters at one place and leaves at one place.

**Ingestion.** The algorithm entry at `0x18f38` calls `FUN_00016c04(gs_buf, gs_len, 1 << sens)` with
the sensitivity from `__GS_SENSITIVITY_CONFIG__`. That function walks the FIFO three shorts at a
time, divides each axis by the scale, takes `sqrt(x^2 + y^2 + z^2)` - the **magnitude**, the
rotation-invariant quantity - and folds it into five words by Welford's algorithm:

    [0]  M2, the running sum of squared deviations
    [1]  running mean
    [2]  max
    [3]  min
    [4]  range, max - min

That is the entire representation. The individual axes are not kept, the time series is not kept,
and nothing is retained that could describe the shape of an artefact.

**Classification.** `FUN_00017820` turns those five words into one of six levels:

    M2 <= thr_lo                 ->  1 if (max - min) < thr_range else 2      still
    thr_lo < M2 <= thr_hi        ->  4, or 8 if mean >= thr_mean              moderate
    M2 > thr_hi                  ->  0x10 if max < thr_max else 0x20          heavy

The five thresholds are host-settable through the parameter setter at `FUN_000151e8`, which writes
them at offsets 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 and 0xa of a table whose pointer lives in .bss - so their values are
not in the image and an exact clone of the classifier is not available from the binary alone.

**Consumption.** `FUN_00017890` is the only caller. It branches on the level and runs a dwell timer:
while the wearer stays in a level, a counter accumulates the elapsed time, and when it passes a
threshold in whole seconds the algorithm copies a fresh set of per-channel values into its working
state and resets the counter. In other words the motion level decides *when to re-baseline*, not
what to subtract.

Counting the readers is the shortest proof. Exactly four functions touch the statistics: the one
that writes them, the classifier, that dwell machine, and a reset. The accelerometer never meets
the optical waveform.

#### So the thing to implement is not what was attempted

Three versions of time-domain cancellation were written against this file and all three made a worn
measurement worse; `motion_clean` in `ppgd.c` carries the numbers. They were the wrong shape of
answer, and this is why. The vendor is not cancelling an artefact out of the pulse - they are
deciding whether to believe the pulse at all, and when to start again.

That is the same family as what this code already does with `motion=` and `swing=`, so the gap is
narrower than it looked. What theirs has and ours does not is the descriptor set - a variance rather
than a median of per-burst magnitudes, plus min, max and range - and the dwell, so that a level has
to persist before it counts as a change of state.

One thing that fell out of reading the reset: `FUN_000172b8` derives the timing from register
`0x0016` as `0x8000 / 327`, rounded to a multiple of 25, which is **100 Hz**. Both sides sample at
the same rate, which retires the last version of the "they run at 25 Hz" idea for good.

### What actually moves the level: it was saturation hiding it

The sweep above said the level does not follow the value written to `0x0118`, because frozen at
`0x1818`, `0x2020`, `0x2828`, `0x4f44` and `0x5620` every run reported the same dc1 and the same
railed dc2. `RAWDUMP` settles it, and the answer is that the observation was real and the reading
of it was wrong.

Sample by sample, over a fifteen-second run:

    plain     start  ch1 3,203,689   ch2 3,210,579     <- railed
              50%    ch1 3,168,599   ch2 3,158,892     <- off the rail
              27% of ch2 samples at the rail

    frozen    start  ch1 3,206,888   ch2 3,210,579
              50%    ch1 3,205,765   ch2 3,210,578
              end    ch1 3,207,892   ch2 3,210,577     <- railed throughout
              67% of ch2 samples at the rail

**Channel 2 is railed from the first sample of every run.** What differs is that a plain run's gain
loop walks it off - five steps that day, `0x2828` down to `0x149a` - and a frozen run cannot. All
the values tried were high enough to clip, and one clipped channel reads exactly like another, so
the level looked independent of the gain when it was only pinned to the top of the range.

So what moves the level is the gain, and our loop stops the moment channel 2 stops clipping, which
is the least signal that is not clipping. Goodix's own AGC holds a channel between half and nine
tenths of the span and steers to seven tenths; ours settles near 31% of it.

### The register comparison, done properly

The earlier diff compared what each side *writes*, which cannot see a register one side never
touches. `tools/gh3011/regdump.c` reads instead, while a measurement is running, and configures
nothing. Reads are taken three at a time - the wanted register, a different one, the wanted register
again - because this bus returns the previous transaction's value often enough that a plain sweep
reported sixty-five differences of which nearly all were an echo of `0x57e4` on one side and `0x0446`
on the other.

Read that way, with both sides measuring, **162 registers compared and two differ**:

| register | vendor | ours | |
|---|---|---|---|
| `0x0118` / `0x0122` | `4f46` | `2323` | the gain |
| `0x0100` | `f530` | `ea60` | three consistent dumps each side |

Seven more move between consecutive dumps and are live rather than configuration: `0x004a`, `0x004c`,
`0x004e`, `0x0086`, `0x008a`, `0x0140`, and `0x0016` intermittently.

That last one is worth naming, because it was briefly a finding. A single dump read `0x0016 = 0057`
against the vendor's `0147` and made a tidy story - four times their sample rate, which our
`hz=99.7` seemed to agree with. Two further dumps read `0147`. It was an echo, the rates are the
same, and the story was wrong.

#### `0x0100`, and three explanations that do not survive

`0x0100` is the one configuration register left. The note in `ppgd.c` said the part clamps what we
write, clamps against the sample rate, and that their sequence writes the `0x0100` block before
setting `0x0016` where ours writes it after. Taken from the wire, that ordering is simply not there:

    vendor   7.106  W 01 00 f5 30        ours   0.839  W 01 00 f5 30
             7.114  W 00 16 01 47               0.848  W 00 16 01 47

One write each, same values, same order, and `0x0016` reads back `0147` on both while they run.

Nor is it the trailing block we write and they do not - `NOVENDORSLOTS=1` removes the rewrite of
`0x0130`/`0x0132`/`0x0134`/`0x0186` and the light registers `0x0080`/`0x0082`/`0x0084`, and `0x0100`
still reads `ea60`. Nor does it follow the gain: frozen at `0x2828`, `0x4f44` and `0x1414` it reads
`ea60` every time.

As signed values ours is exactly twice theirs, -5536 against -2768. That is where it stands.

One other difference the ordered diff turned up and which nothing has been made of yet: our start
runs the whole `0182`/`0180`/`0064`/`006a` preamble three times where theirs runs it once.

### Movement: we reject it, they subtract it, and our masking is not running

Asked whether the saturation is compensated for movement, the answer is no, and less than the code
suggests.

For the ratio, movement is only ever a veto. `ratio_usable` wants a minimum amplitude on each
channel, the windows have to agree, and a moving wrist returns `no_agreement` and no number. The
waveform the ratio is computed from is never cleaned; it is accepted or discarded whole.

The vendor subtracts. `spo2_r_value` comes out of a call whose first arguments are the g-sensor
FIFO, and their own log line says how much of it: `spo2 calc, gs_len=40` on every FIFO event, one a
second. Forty accelerometer samples per second, handed to the same function that produces the
ratio.

There is motion masking here, but for the rate rather than the ratio: candidate heart rates that
coincide with a peak in the accelerometer spectrum are discounted, which is what stops an arm
swinging at 100 a minute being read as a pulse of 100. **It is not running.** It needs
`naccel > 64` before it will even compute the accelerometer's sample rate, and a twenty-second
saturation pass collects eleven samples:

    asamples=11 nmotion=21 accel_fd=4        (2100 optical samples, 21 seconds)

Eleven samples in twenty-one seconds is about half a hertz, against their forty. So `afs` is zero,
the penalty scale `aref` stays zero, and the discount is arithmetically absent from every
measurement. Eighty times undersampled, and silently inactive rather than weak.

That ordering matters for anyone continuing this: compensation is not the next step. Sampling the
accelerometer at a rate that could support it is, and until that lands the existing masking is not
doing anything either.

#### And a wear check ppgd does not make

The same off-wrist run reported `hr=60 spread=0 ... ac1=2 ac2=4 r=0.782` from a light level of 905
counts - a confident-looking rate and ratio from a sensor lying on a desk. `vitalsd` asks `adtwear`
first and would not have published it, so nothing downstream is wrong, but `ppgd` on its own will
answer about a wrist that is not there.

### The saturation curve, exactly

`110 - 25R` was fitted here from measurements. It is now readable in their binary.

`FUN_000207c4` maps an array of ratios through a quadratic, elementwise, with coefficients taken
from the algorithm's config struct:

    out[i] = cfg[0x2c] * R[i] * R[i] + cfg[0x30] * R[i] + cfg[0x34]

The defaults are written by the initialiser at `0x1f8c0`, from three floats lying together in the
image at file offsets `0xfc7c`, `0xfc80` and the word before them:

    cfg+0x2c = 0.0        the square term
    cfg+0x30 = -25.0      the slope
    cfg+0x34 = 110.0      the intercept

So the curve is a line, the fit was right about the slope, and six points out on the intercept. Each
default is guarded by a flag byte - `cfg+8` for these three - which the host sets when it overrides
them; the published example's `{0x2000..0x2005}` block is that override, and this watch's daemon
never writes it, so these are the constants in use.

Beside them in the same pool, and belonging to the same initialiser, are the thresholds:

    85.0  50.0  95.0  90.0  75.0        saturation bounds
    0.1   0.2   0.08  0.05              quality thresholds
    20.0  10.0  3.0                     motion thresholds
    100 (int)                           the window, in samples

## How this software is put together

Three programs and a socket.

`ppgd` does one measurement and exits. It configures the part, polls the FIFO, and prints a line
of fields - rate, the DC and AC of both channels, the ratio, a pressure when the pulse shape
supports one, and the confidence behind each. Everything that reads a waveform is in here, and it
holds no state between runs beyond an optional previous rate to help it choose between two
candidate frequencies on a moving wrist.

`adtwear` answers one question - is this on a wrist - and exits. It arms the chip's detector and
waits for the interrupt.

`vitalsd` is the only long-lived part. It runs in the init slot the vendor's daemon used, listens
on an abstract unix socket named `watchvitals`, and answers three requests by running the other
two as helpers:

    ->  hr            green LED, heart rate
    ->  spo2          red and infrared, adds the ratio and the pulse shape
    ->  wear          the wear detector alone, no measurement

    <-  hr=49 spread=2 hz=24.9 ... r=0.706 sbp=113 dbp=69 conf=0.82 temp=32.6
    <-  hr=0 reason=no_agreement
    <-  worn=1 adt=1

The helpers are separate processes rather than functions because the measurement needs
`/dev/gh_tools` to itself and takes the better part of a minute; a daemon holding that open cannot
also hand it to something else, and a crash in a measurement should not take the socket with it.

A refusal is a normal answer and says why. `weak=1` on a ratio means there was too little pulse
behind it to divide by; `tracked=1` on a rate means the windows disagreed and the previous rate
chose between them; `reason=not_worn` means the wear check answered before any LED was lit.

The client is an Android launcher which speaks to that socket and knows nothing about the sensor
beyond those three words.

### How a rate is arrived at

Six-second windows, a Goertzel filter over the plausible band, and the magnitudes averaged. A rate
is published when the windows agree; when they disagree the previous rate breaks the tie, with
asymmetric bounds - a wrist can speed up faster than it slows down. Green carries the rate because
the physics is not close: haemoglobin absorbs green strongly, and measured on this part green
swings 200 to 900 counts where red manages 1 to 80. Green also only reaches the surface
capillaries, which is what makes it better under motion - deep tissue moves, shallow capillaries
move less.

Confidence is a Gaussian probability that the true rate lies within tolerance of the estimate,
from the spread across windows, with the standard error floored so a lucky pair of agreeing
windows cannot claim certainty.

## What the measurements are worth

| | how it was checked | result |
|---|---|---|
| heart rate | against a cuff's pulse readout, and against the vendor's own daemon on the same wrist minutes apart | about +1 bpm, three of four measurements within two |
| wear detection | fifty runs on and off a wrist | worn within about a second every time on, silence every time off, no overlap |
| wrist temperature | a thermopile, against a room and a wrist | reads 34.6 on a wrist against 26.5 on a table; it is a wrist and not a core temperature |
| blood pressure | against thirty cuff readings over two days | tracks poorly - see below |
| saturation | nothing adequate | a ratio, not a percentage - see below |

### Heart rate

This is the one worth trusting. Compared against the vendor's daemon on the same wrist minutes
apart it read 46 to 52 where the vendor read 49, and against a cuff's own pulse figure it sits
about a beat high. It refuses rather than guesses: when the six-second windows disagree it returns
`hr=0 reason=no_agreement`, which is what a moving wrist looks like, and roughly what the vendor
does in the same situation.

### Blood pressure

Uncalibrated and, more importantly, insensitive. Thirty cuff readings taken alongside it spanned
94/66 to 121/73, and the estimate stayed near 114 systolic throughout - so it is not a pressure
that is offset by a constant, it is a pressure that barely moves. It is published only when the
pulse shape behind it is physiological - an upstroke between 80 and 250 ms and a positive
augmentation index - and both numbers are withheld when it is not, because half a pressure is not
a reading. Treat it as a shape metric with pressure-like units rather than a measurement of
anyone's blood pressure.

### Saturation

No percentage is published, deliberately. The ratio of ratios is real and behaves - around 0.5 to
0.9 with a pulse to measure - but turning it into a number out of a hundred needs a fingertip
reference this had no access to. A single anchor was tried and printed 86% for a healthy wearer at
rest: alarming, and with no measurement behind it.

The ratio also drifts by a factor of three over eight hours on a motionless wrist, which makes any
one-point calibration wrong by several points within a day. So what the daemon reports is movement
away from this sensor's own recent baseline, on the reasoning that the drift is slow and a
desaturation is not - an apnoea lasts tens of seconds and the instrument takes hours to wander
that far. A fall is worth seeing. The absolute number is an assumption and is not a measurement of
anyone's saturation.

### Why there is no saturation percentage, measured against a meter

A fingertip pulse oximeter was finally put beside this, which was the missing reference every
earlier note blamed. It is not what was missing.

With the wearer at a constant 99% on the meter, our ratio came back 1.435, 1.668, 1.692, 1.698 and
1.780 across consecutive passes, and 1.253, 1.436 and 1.514 at the vendor's own gain. A pass taken
during a breath hold read 1.470 - inside the resting spread, and indistinguishable from it.

The cause is in every line of output: `ac1` is about ten counts and `ac2` about seven.

Not against the 3.17 million the DC reads, which is mostly pedestal - raising the gain from 0x2828
to 0x6060 moves the DC by half a percent, which is how you can tell. The dark code is three units
of 0x100000, so the light-derived level is about 25,000 counts and the perfusion index is near
0.04%. A fingertip oximeter works at 0.5% or better, so this is ten to a hundred times low rather
than a thousand.

That is still enough to sink it. R is `(ac1/l1)/(ac2/l2)`, so one ADC count on an amplitude of
seven moves it by about 14%, where a 98 to 92 desaturation moves it by about 12%. **The
quantisation is comparable to the signal**, which is also why R reads 1.7 where physiology allows
at most about 1.0: the number has a ratio's units and noise's content.

Gain does not rescue it. Across 0x2828, 0x4040 and 0x6060 the amplitude went 29.2, 28.8 and 0.1 -
that register is not a clean multiplier on this part. The only control that moved real light was
0x0136, which lifted the level from 3.47M to 3.73M with the gain frozen, and the pulse extraction
then failed for other reasons. Raising the light without losing the pulse is where this stands.

The second reason is the wearer. Across an entire breath hold the meter stayed between 96 and 99 -
a healthy person at rest cannot desaturate enough to define a slope, and a slope needs two points
at different saturations. One anchor fixes an offset and nothing else, which is how an earlier
attempt published 86% for someone who was fine.

So a meter does not unblock this. What would is more light: the pulse is ten counts partly because
the red and infrared LEDs are driven gently, and `0x0136` and `0x0080` set that. If drive can lift
the amplitude to fifty or a hundred counts, quantisation stops dominating and the question is worth
asking again. `RSERIES=1` on ppgd emits the ratio window by window, which is the shape a
calibration against a moving reference needs.

What the meter did settle is the heart rate: ours read 55 to 60 against its 57 to 67 across the
same passes, including during the hold when the meter's rate rose and ours followed.

### Motion

Green is used for the rate partly because it survives movement better - it only reaches the
surface capillaries, and deep tissue moves more than shallow tissue does. Beyond that the
defences are a spectral purity test, which rejects a window whose peak is not clearly a peak, and
an asymmetric tie-break against the previous rate. None of this makes a reading during vigorous
movement trustworthy; it makes the code decline one.

### A caution

This is a reverse-engineered driver for an undocumented part in a cheap watch, checked against a
consumer blood-pressure cuff. It is not a medical device and nothing here was validated against
one. The heart rate is good enough to be interesting; the rest is recorded honestly so that
nobody has to rediscover which parts are not.

## The route this replaced, and why it is gone

Before the daemon existed the launcher read this chip from Java, underneath the sensor HAL and
through the vendor's own library. That code has been deleted; what it established is here,
because it is why the daemon is shaped the way it is.

### The sensor HAL does not deliver

During a measurement the framework shows the sensor activated and connected, and then sits at
`First flush pending` for the whole window without producing a single sample:

    gh30x_sensor  handle=0x00000008, active-count=1, connections=1
    gh30x_sensor | status: First flush pending | pending flush events 0
    last=< 60.0,120.0, 79.0>        unchanged across 29 s and all ten samples taken

That stale triple is the reason readings were identical for hours, and the reason `com.ic.work`
reported high, low, heart and oxygen all as zero: it is handed nothing, so it has nothing to
report. Neither the wear check nor its work queue was ever the cause - both were patched, and
with the wear flag forced true on a worn watch the zeros survived.

The same applies to the thermometer: `GXTS02S` in the platform sensor list is a mirror sitting
at `last=<0.0,0.0,0.0>`, and `registerListener` on it returns that for ever.

### The driver underneath is fine

Reading `/dev/input/event1` across the same measurement gives a value every second on a worn
watch:

    REL_RX  0x395c -> 57 bpm,  92 %     sensor settling
    REL_RX  0x3916 -> 57 bpm,  22 %
    REL_RX  0x3a50 -> 58 bpm,  80 %
    REL_RX  0x3b64 -> 59 bpm, 100 %     converged, and steady to the end

So a reading is two bytes packed into one `REL_RX`: heart rate high, saturation low. `REL_RZ` is
a once-a-second progress tick (`0x0101`, `0x0201` ... `0x1d01`) and carries no measurement. The
node is world readable, `crwxrwxrwx`.

Blood pressure is not in that stream. The HAL's three-value event has one, so the vendor's
library computes it above the driver.

### Only the vendor could start it, and only in the wrong mode

Nothing in userspace could start the sensor: `goodix_health` exposes no sysfs control, there is
no character device, and the i2c protocol is the vendor's. So the measurement had to be started
by `com.ic.work` through the HAL - the piece that wedges - and only restarting that service
cleared it. Three times in two days, each costing hours of pressure and saturation while the
driver went on producing a pulse perfectly well.

`libICJniUtils.so` exports `enableSPO2` as a GLOBAL symbol with no
`Java_com_ic_jni_ICJniUtils_` wrapper, so it cannot be reached from Java however the class is
declared; a small JNI shim that dlopened the library and called it directly was the way in.
`enablePPG`, which *is* wrapped, starts the chip in heart rate mode only - `event ppg 59 ,
spo2 0 , weared 1` on every sample of a full window, which is what made the vendor's service
look indispensable.

Activating through the HAL is what selected saturation mode: `command, GH_30X gh30x_Spo2Start`
is a string in `sensors.sl8521e.so`, not in `libICJniUtils.so`. So the arrangement was to
register a listener that was never expected to hear anything, purely for the activation, and
read the samples off the input device instead.

### Why none of that is needed now

This daemon talks to the part over i2c itself, in the init slot the vendor's daemon occupied.
There is no HAL in the path to wedge, no service to restart, and no library to borrow an
undocumented entry point from. The last thing the vendor's library was still used for was
reading the thermometer, and the `temp` request replaced that too.

One thing did survive the deletion and is worth keeping in view: the pressures were the only
measurement that came from above the driver, computed inside `getHighBloodPressure` during the
vendor's own measurement. This code derives them from the pulse shape instead, which is a
different method reaching a similar number, and `## What the measurements are worth` above is
the honest account of what that is worth.

## How the vendor computes a saturation

Read out of `gh3011_service` with Ghidra. Not complete - the conversion from a ratio to a
percentage is still not isolated - but enough of it is settled to be worth writing down, and
several parts contradict what we assumed.

### The path

    mode 7 in the interrupt dispatcher      FUN_000121c8
      -> FUN_00018f38                        builds a 124-byte config, then loops
        -> FUN_00016a3c                      once per sample
          -> FUN_00022928                    the saturation itself, 2178 bytes
          -> FUN_0001d79c                    a second result, 660 bytes

The library announces itself as `PV_SPO2_v1.8.7.06` under Goodix's `hbd_ctrl`, and logs twelve
values per calculation: `spo2 calc, gs_len=%d, result=%d,...` twelve times over.

### It is not a neural network

The whole path uses `__aeabi_fmul`, `fadd`, `fsub`, `fdiv`, the float comparisons, and `sqrtf`.
There is no `logf`, no `expf`, no `powf`, and no matrix arithmetic anywhere in it. Whatever the
vendor's stack does elsewhere, the saturation is ordinary arithmetic.

What it does have is 182 float comparisons across the path, which is the shape of a piecewise
function or a set of quality gates rather than a formula evaluated once.

### The parts that are settled

**It smooths exactly as the rate does.** The same 0.8/0.2 blend we already implement, with the same
escape:

    if (|new - old| < 10)  result = new*0.8 + old*0.2;   else  result = new;

There is a second, slower coefficient of 0.05 elsewhere in the path.

**It is clamped at 70.** In FUN_00016a3c, immediately before the result is stored:

    if (local_50 - 1U < 0x45) local_50 = 0x46;

Anything computed between 1 and 69 is reported as 70. So a low vendor reading is not a measurement
of a low saturation - it is the floor, and their display cannot distinguish 70 from 40.

**The twelve outputs are fixed-point.** Scaled by 10, 100, 10000 and 100000 on the way out, so the
diagnostics beside the percentage are ratios and levels rather than more percentages.

### The formula

`FUN_00020040` is the conversion. It accumulates a weighted mean over the window - `sum(w*x)/sum(x)`
- and then evaluates a quadratic on it:

    r     = sum(w[i] * x[i]) / sum(x[i])
    spo2  = coef[0x2c] * r * r  +  coef[0x30] * r  +  coef[0x34]

`FUN_0001f8c0` fills those three slots, and only when the block has not already been configured:

    puVar6[0x0b] = 0.0      /* byte offset 0x2c - the r-squared term */
    puVar6[0x0c] = -25.0    /* byte offset 0x30 - the r term        */
    puVar6[0x0d] = 110.0    /* byte offset 0x34 - the constant      */

So the shipped curve is

    SpO2 = 110 - 25 * R

with the quadratic term zero. That is the textbook empirical calibration, and it is a default
rather than a fit: the guard means a calibration written into the block beforehand would survive.
There is no per-device calibration anywhere in the daemon that writes it.

The same initialiser sets the bounds the result is held to - 85, 95, 90, 75 and 50 - along with
0.1 and 0.2, and a set of small counters (15, 3, 5, 10, 10, 10, 8, 2) which are window lengths.

### What this means for ours

Our ratio is computed the same way in principle, and the curve is one line. Nothing in their
saturation is unavailable to us: no table, no network, no per-device constants. Applying their
curve to a measured R of 0.47 gives 98.2%, and to 0.7 gives 92.5% - both plausible, which is the
first time any of this has produced a number worth printing.

What stops it being useful is upstream and already documented above: R itself moves between 0.3 and
1.8 on the same wrist within minutes, and 110 - 25R turns that into saturations from 102% down to
65%. The vendor has the same problem and hides it - the result is clamped to a floor of 70 before
being reported, so their display cannot show what a bad R would produce.

## Why the pressure does not track, measured

The shape behind the pressure is now reported with a measure of whether the beats agreed about it:
`sutmed` is the upstroke as the individual beats measured it, `sutmad` how far they sat from that,
`sutn` how many were measured. The pressure itself still uses the ensemble figure.

That measure is real. Over 108 archived recordings replayed offline, it predicts how far the
ensemble sits from what the beats actually said:

    sutmad     n    ensemble differs from the beats by
    0-20      25    6.8 ms
    21-40     12    23.5 ms
    41-80     22    48.1 ms
    81+       12    95.3 ms

So a pass can now say whether its own beats agreed, which they often do not.

Two things were tried on the strength of it and neither is worth having. Using the per-beat median
instead of the ensemble made the published pressure slightly *less* consistent - standard deviation
5.43 mmHg against 5.29. Publishing only where the beats agreed, `sutmad <= 40`, tightened the range

The reason both failed is in the arithmetic. Decomposing `sbp = 100 + 0.28*bpm - 0.055*sut + 11*ai`
across the 71 recordings that produced a pressure:

    0.28*bpm      sd 3.62 mmHg
    -0.055*sut    sd 2.84 mmHg
    11*ai         sd 2.84 mmHg
    total         sd 5.29 mmHg, range 32.6

The largest single source of movement is the heart rate, not the shape. The estimate's spread is
about the size of the real variation - the cuff spanned 94 to 121 systolic over the same two days,
27 mmHg - so it is not that the number fails to move. It moves about the right amount and not in
step, which is a different problem, and the earlier cuff comparison is the direct evidence: the
estimate sat near 114 whether the wearer was at 105 or at 120.

A more consistent upstroke cannot fix that. What would be needed is a calibration against paired
cuff readings, and the sensitivity to check whether this formula can track a real change at all -
neither of which a tighter shape provides. The agreement measure stays because it is cheap and
says something true; the estimator and the gate do not, because they were measured and did not
help.
